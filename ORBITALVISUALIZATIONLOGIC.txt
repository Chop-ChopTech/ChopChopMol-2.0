How the MO Visualization Script Works (Plain English)The Big PictureA molecular orbital (MO) is a mathematical function that tells you the probability of finding an electron at any point in 3D space around a molecule. The script's job is to read the math from a file and draw a 3D picture of where the electron is likely to be found.
Step 1: Read the Recipe (Load the Molden File)The molden file is like a recipe card that contains:
Where the atoms are (oxygen at the center, two hydrogens nearby)
Building block formulas (Gaussian functions - bell curves in 3D)
Mixing instructions (coefficients that say "take 99% of this, subtract 3% of that...")
PySCF reads this recipe and gives us all the ingredients we need.
Step 2: Set Up a 3D Measuring GridImagine placing the molecule inside a transparent box and filling that box with an invisible 3D grid of measurement points - like a 3D graph paper with 50×50×50 = 125,000 tiny dots.Each dot is a location where we'll ask: "What's the value of the orbital here?"
Step 3: Calculate the Orbital Value at Every PointFor each of the 125,000 grid points, we calculate the orbital's value using the recipe from the molden file:In plain terms:> "At this point in space, add up all the Gaussian bell curves (centered on different atoms), each multiplied by its coefficient from the recipe."
Some coefficients are positive (add electron density)
Some are negative (subtract electron density)
The result is a number at each point: positive, negative, or near zero
Step 4: Find the "Skin" of the Orbital (Marching Cubes)Now we have 125,000 numbers filling our 3D box. To visualize this, we pick a threshold value (like 0.02) and find all the surfaces where the orbital equals that value.Think of it like this:> "If the orbital were a cloud, draw the surface where the cloud has a certain density."The marching cubes algorithm walks through the grid, cube by cube, and figures out where the surface passes through - like finding the contour lines on a topographic map, but in 3D.We do this twice:
+0.02 → finds the positive lobe (where electrons have one phase)
-0.02 → finds the negative lobe (opposite phase)
Step 5: Draw the PictureFinally, we use Plotly to assemble a 3D scene:
Blue surface = positive lobe of the orbital
Red surface = negative lobe of the orbital
Colored spheres = atoms (red for oxygen, white for hydrogen)
Gray lines = bonds connecting atoms
The result is saved as an HTML file that opens in your browser, where you can rotate and zoom the 3D model.
Analogy: Sculpting from a BlockThink of it like sculpting:
Molden file = Blueprint with measurements
3D grid = A block of clay divided into tiny cubes
Calculating values = Marking each tiny cube as "keep" or "remove" based on the blueprint
Marching cubes = Carving away everything outside the surface
Plotly = Painting and displaying the finished sculpture
Why Two Colors?Orbitals have phase (like waves have peaks and troughs):
Blue (+) and Red (-) don't mean positive/negative charge
They represent the mathematical sign of the wavefunction
Where blue meets red, electrons cancel out (that's a node)
This phase information is crucial for understanding chemical bonding
